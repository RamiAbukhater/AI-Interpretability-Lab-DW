How this works

In Homework 1 you rebuilt the paper's main results on a smaller model: you found the Assistant Axis, watched conversations drift along it, and steered the model with it. Reproducing a result is the first half of research. The second half is asking a question nobody has answered yet. The ideas below are grouped by theme: multi-agent, safety, cognitive science, and mechanism and models. Each card says what the idea asks, how you would do it in a few steps, what makes it hard, and which part of the notebook it builds on. Pick one as a team, or propose your own. Either way, your proposal has to answer the checklist at the bottom of this page.

Read before choosing: constraints that apply to every idea
  1. Open weights only. The method reads internal activations, so it only works on models you run yourself. API models such as GPT or Claude return text, not hidden states. They can be a conversation partner or a judge, but they cannot be measured.
  2. Memory. On Kaggle's T4 x2 (two 16 GB GPUs) you can fit one Gemma 2 9B, or two Gemma 2 2B models at once, but not two 9B models. Larger models need 4-bit quantization or a different machine.
  3. Axes from different models cannot be compared directly. Each model has its own hidden size and its own axis, so compare normalized projections or rankings, not raw vectors (why).
  4. Confounds. A direction found with PCA might reflect tone, formality, or response length rather than persona. Most projects need a control showing that is not what is being measured.
  5. Judge cost and noise. Every experiment graded by an LLM judge adds OpenRouter spend, and judges make mistakes, so spot-check judge scores by hand.
Recommended starting points
  1. Flagship team: ideas 1 and 2 combined (multi-agent drift and persona contagion). Novel, visual, and builds directly on the drift tools from Section 2. The paper's multi-turn conversations always pair one measured model with a simulated user; it never measures two models talking to each other.
  2. Cognitive science team: idea 7 (personality structure in persona space).
  3. On-ramp for newer members: idea 12 (robustness across models and compression). Least creative, but most likely to produce a clean result.
  4. Safety team: idea 4 (jailbreak early warning). Strongest safety result, but only for a team that follows the content guidelines in that card.

The twelve ideas

Show:

1 Two models talking to each other

Multi-agentIntermediateTeam of 3 to 4

Question. When two models hold a long conversation, does either drift away from the Assistant, and does one pull the other?

Method.
  1. Run long conversations between two models (or one model playing both sides).
  2. Project each speaker's turns onto its own Assistant Axis with ConversationAnalyzer.
  3. Plot both trajectories over turns and compare them.
Constraints.
  • The cheapest version is one model playing both sides, which needs only one model in memory. Two different model families needs two small models (for example Gemma 2 2B and Qwen 3 1.7B or 4B) or a larger GPU.
  • Model-to-model conversations often collapse into loops or mutual flattery after a few dozen turns. That could itself be a finding, but the team needs a way to tell drift apart from degeneration (for example, a repetition measure).
  • Use several random seeds and opening prompts. One conversation is an anecdote.

Builds on Section 2: drift monitoring and hooks (concept 6).

2 Persona contagion

Multi-agentAdvancedTeam of 3 to 4

Question. If a stable Assistant talks with a model pinned to a strong persona (for example a pirate or a conspiracy theorist), does the Assistant drift toward that persona?

Method.
  1. Pair a normal Assistant with a persona-prompted partner.
  2. Track both speakers' projections onto the axis, turn by turn.
  3. Use lagged correlation to test whether one model's drift follows the other's.
Constraints.
  • Causality is hard. Drift might come from the conversation's topics rather than the partner's persona, so include a control partner that covers the same topics with a neutral persona.

Builds on Section 2: drift monitoring (concept 6).

3 Agents with assigned roles

Multi-agentAdvancedTeam of 3 to 5

Question. Multi-agent frameworks give models roles like critic, CEO, or adversary. Does role assignment pull agents off the axis, and does that predict failures or broken rules?

Method.
  1. Run a small multi-agent task with assigned roles.
  2. Project each agent's activations onto the axis.
  3. Compare projections against task success and rule-following.
Constraints.
  • Needs a task with a clear success measure, which is a big setup cost. Best for a team with agent-framework experience.
  • Every agent you want to measure has to be an open-weights model you run yourself (see the shared constraints), so keep agents small or let one model play every role.

Builds on Section 1: persona vectors (concept 3), and Section 2: drift monitoring (concept 6).

4 The axis as an early warning for jailbreaks

SafetyIntermediateTeam of 2 to 4

Question. Do jailbreak prompts push activations off the Assistant end of the axis before the model produces anything harmful?

Method.
  1. Take jailbreak prompts and benign prompts from a public benchmark.
  2. Project the activations on each prompt (before any response is generated) onto the axis.
  3. Treat the projection as a classifier score and report an ROC curve and its area.
Content guidelines for this project
  • Use existing public benchmark prompts only: JailbreakBench (its JBB-Behaviors dataset has matched harmful and benign behaviors, which suits an ROC curve) or HarmBench. Both are public and MIT-licensed.
  • Do not write new harmful prompts.
  • Do not publish or share harmful model completions, including in slides, notebooks you make public, or chat. Report scores and aggregate results only.
  • Prefer measuring the prompt before generation, so you rarely need to produce completions at all.
Constraints.
  • Role-play jailbreaks will obviously move the projection. The interesting question is whether non-role-play attacks move it too, so report the two kinds separately.

Builds on projection onto the axis: Section 1 (concept 5) and Section 2 (concept 6).

5 Activation capping on Gemma 2 9B

SafetyAdvancedTeam of 2 to 3

Question. The notebook skips activation capping because the original exercise relies on the paper's precomputed settings for Qwen 3 32B, which does not fit on Kaggle. Can capping be rebuilt on 9B, and what does it cost in helpfulness?

Method.
  1. Implement the paper's cap (Section 5, Equation 1), which only intervenes when the projection falls below a threshold.
  2. Calibrate thresholds and layers for 9B. The paper sets the cap at the 25th percentile of normal projections.
  3. Measure drift prevention and general capability, each with and without capping.
Constraints.
  • Calibration is fiddly. ARENA notes that capping with a single generic axis works poorly compared with the paper's per-layer calibrated vectors.
  • Needs two evaluations, one for drift and one for capability, so the team can show capping does not just make the model worse.

Builds on Section 2: steering (concept 7).

6 Who the user is

SafetyIntermediateTeam of 2 to 3

Question. Keeping the model's own persona fixed, does the user's identity (a child, an expert, someone in distress, a flatterer) move the model along the axis, and does that track sycophancy?

Method.
  1. Hold the system prompt fixed and vary only the user's described identity.
  2. Measure projections onto the axis for each identity.
  3. Score responses for sycophancy with a judge and compare.
Constraints.
  • The team must define up front how to separate "the model adapts its tone", which is fine, from "the model loses its identity", which is the worrying case.
  • The paper varies user behaviour to create realistic conversations, but never treats user identity as the variable under study, so this is new ground.

Builds on Section 2: drift monitoring (concept 6).

7 Personality structure in persona space

Cognitive scienceIntermediateTeam of 2 to 3

Question. The paper focuses on the first principal component. Do the next components (PC2 through PC5) line up with human personality dimensions such as the Big Five? This connects to the lexical hypothesis in psychology, the idea that important personality differences end up encoded in everyday language.

Method.
  1. Fit PCA with at least five components (the notebook uses two).
  2. Have a judge rate each persona on the Big Five traits.
  3. Correlate those ratings with each persona's position on PC2 through PC5.
Constraints.
  • Interpreting components is subjective. Decide in advance what counts as a match, and compare against shuffled ratings as a baseline.
  • About 20 personas is few for five components. Expect to add personas, or use the paper's 275 roles via USE_FULL_PERSONA_LIST if you have the GPU time.
  • The paper only gives loose readings of PC2 and PC3 (Section 2.2, Table 1; for Gemma, PC2 is roughly "informal or creative" versus "systematic") and never tests the Big Five, so this is new.

Builds on Section 1: PCA and the Assistant Axis (concept 5).

8 Narrator distance

Cognitive scienceIntermediateTeam of 2 to 3

Question. Does writing a character in third person ("the villain said") keep the model nearer the Assistant end than writing it in first person ("I am the villain")? This gets at the difference between simulating a character and becoming one.

Method.
  1. Build matched prompts that differ only in perspective.
  2. Extract response activations as in Section 1 and project them onto the axis.
  3. Compare the two perspectives, persona by persona.
Constraints.
  • Prompts must be tightly matched, and the content of the responses must stay comparable, or the comparison is not fair.

Builds on Section 1: persona vectors (concept 3 and concept 4).

9 Language and culture

Cognitive scienceIntermediateTeam of 2 to 4, ideally with native speakers

Question. If the axis is built from English prompts, does it still separate personas in Spanish, Mandarin, or Arabic? Is the Assistant one character across languages, or a different one in each?

Method.
  1. Translate the personas and questions.
  2. Extract activations and project them onto the English axis.
  3. Also build a separate axis in each language and compare it with the English one by cosine similarity (same model, so this is allowed).
Constraints.
  • Judge quality drops outside English, and Gemma's ability varies by language.
  • Translated prompts need review by native speakers.

Builds on Section 1: persona vectors and PCA (concept 3 and concept 5).

10 Base versus instruction-tuned models

Mechanism and modelsIntermediateTeam of 2 to 3

Question. The paper already looked at base models for Gemma 2 27B, but always started from the instruction-tuned model: it reused the -it model's axis to steer the base model (Section 3.2.2), and ran PCA on base-model activations of text the -it model wrote, finding nearly the same top components (Appendix B.3). Does an Assistant direction emerge in gemma-2-9b from its own completions, with no help from gemma-2-9b-it? That bears directly on the claim that post-training selects a character that was already there.

Method.
  1. Generate persona responses from the base model itself, using completion-style prompts.
  2. Build persona space and an axis in the base model, and separately in the -it model.
  3. Compare the two axes by cosine similarity, layer by layer, and compare persona rankings.
Constraints.
  • Base models do not follow chat formatting, so completion-style prompts are needed (the paper used prefills such as "My job is to"), which makes the comparison less clean.
  • Base and -it share the same hidden size and start from the same weights, so comparing their raw axes is reasonable here, unlike across model families. Still report rankings too.
  • Read Section 3.2.2 and Appendix B.3 first, so you extend the paper rather than repeat it.

Builds on Section 1: PCA and the Assistant Axis (concept 5).

11 Breaking the axis into SAE features

Mechanism and modelsAdvancedTeam of 2 to 3

Question. What makes up "Assistant-ness"? Which interpretable features, such as refusal, politeness, or self-reference, line up with the axis?

Method.
  1. Load a Gemma Scope sparse autoencoder (SAE) for the layer you extract from.
  2. Find the SAE features whose directions align most with the axis, and the features most active on Assistant versus role-playing responses.
  3. Read and label the top features, using Neuronpedia's feature pages where they exist.
Constraints.
  • Check layer coverage. Gemma Scope has residual-stream SAEs for every layer of the base Gemma 2 2B (layers 0 to 25) and 9B (0 to 41). For the instruction-tuned 9B it has them only at layers 9, 20 and 31, and none for 2B-it. Our notebook extracts at layer 27 (9B) or 17 (2B). Either re-extract the axis at layer 20 or 31 and use the -it SAEs, or use the base-model SAE at your layer and check how well it reconstructs -it activations.
  • Loading an SAE alongside the model is tight on a T4. Use the narrowest width that works (16k features).
  • Labeling features takes a lot of manual reading.

Builds on Section 1: the residual stream (concept 3) and PCA (concept 5).

12 Robustness across models and compression

Mechanism and modelsStarterTeam of 2 to 4

Question. Does the axis survive 4-bit quantization? Does it appear in Gemma 4 or Qwen, and how does its shape compare?

Method.
  1. Rerun the Section 1 pipeline on a 4-bit version of Gemma 2 9B.
  2. Rerun it on other model families that fit on a T4 x2. On Kaggle that includes Gemma 4 E2B, E4B, and 12B (instruction-tuned versions) and Qwen 3 4B and 8B.
  3. Compare the PCA structure (variance explained by PC1) and the persona rankings along each model's axis.
Constraints.
  • Raw vectors cannot be compared across models (see the shared constraints). Compare rankings and variance explained.
  • Other models have different chat templates and layer counts, so check format_messages and pick the extraction layer as a fraction of depth.

Builds on the full Section 1 pipeline (concepts 1 to 5).

What your proposal must answer

Whether you choose a card or your own idea, submit one to two pages per team that answers each item:

  1. Research question, in one sentence.
  2. Which notebook method it builds on (name the section and functions).
  3. Which model, and how it fits in memory on Kaggle's T4 x2.
  4. The control or baseline that rules out the obvious confound.
  5. Rough OpenRouter cost: how many judge calls, which judge model, and an estimate in dollars.
  6. What counts as success or failure: the result that would support your idea, and the result that would count against it.
  7. Team: members, and who owns which part.

A strong proposal changes one thing at a time, makes a prediction that could turn out wrong, includes a comparison, and is small enough to show a first result within two weeks on free hardware.
