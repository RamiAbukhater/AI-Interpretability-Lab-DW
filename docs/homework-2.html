How this works

In Homework 1 you rebuilt the paper's main results on a smaller model: you found the Assistant Axis, watched conversations drift along it, and steered the model with it. Reproducing a result is the first half of research. The second half is asking a question nobody has answered yet. The ideas below are grouped by theme: multi-agent, safety, and cognitive science. The multi-agent idea is one larger project for a single team, which splits into three sub-groups and writes one paper together. Each card says what the idea asks, how you would do it in a few steps, what makes it hard, and which part of the notebook it builds on. Pick one as a team, or propose your own. Either way, your proposal has to answer the checklist at the bottom of this page.

Read before choosing: constraints that apply to every idea
  1. Open weights only. The method reads internal activations, so it only works on models you run yourself. API models such as GPT or Claude return text, not hidden states. They can be a conversation partner or a judge, but they cannot be measured.
  2. Memory. On Kaggle's T4 x2 (two 16 GB GPUs) you can fit one Gemma 2 9B, or two Gemma 2 2B models at once, but not two 9B models. Larger models need 4-bit quantization or a different machine.
  3. Axes from different models cannot be compared directly. Each model has its own hidden size and its own axis, so compare normalized projections or rankings, not raw vectors (why).
  4. Confounds. A direction found with PCA might reflect tone, formality, or response length rather than persona. Most projects need a control showing that is not what is being measured.
  5. Judge cost and noise. Every experiment graded by an LLM judge adds OpenRouter spend, and judges make mistakes, so spot-check judge scores by hand.
Recommended starting points
  1. Flagship team: idea 1 (multi-agent persona dynamics), one paper covering all three multi-agent questions. Starts from a known behavioural result (Li et al., 2024) and takes it inside the model, so it is visual and builds directly on the drift tools from Section 2.
  2. Cognitive science team: idea 4 (personality structure in persona space).
  3. On-ramp for newer members: idea 5 (narrator distance). It reuses the Section 1 pipeline almost unchanged with matched prompts, so it is the most likely to produce a clean result.
  4. Safety team: idea 2 (jailbreak early warning). Strongest safety result, but only for a team that follows the content guidelines in that card.

1 Multi-agent persona dynamics

Multi-agentTeam of 6 to 9, in three sub-groups

One team takes on all three multi-agent questions as parts of a single paper. The three questions share most of their tools: a loop that runs conversations between models, a per-speaker projection onto the axis, a check for degenerate conversations, and a set of seeds and opening prompts. So the team builds those once and then splits into three sub-groups, each owning one focus. Together they tell one story: what happens when models talk (A), whether one model can pull another (B), and what assigned roles do inside a working group of agents (C).

Where this starts. Focus A and B extend Li et al. (2024), who had two system-prompted chatbots talk to each other and found that within about eight rounds the agent stops following its own system prompt and starts taking on its partner's. They measured this from behaviour only, by asking probe questions and scoring the answers ("does it still act like its prompt?"). Their code is public. The new part is looking inside: we found no work that tracks this drift along the Assistant Axis, asks whether the activations move before the behaviour does, or tests whether contagion shows up as movement toward the partner's persona. The Assistant Axis paper itself only ever pairs one measured model with a simulated user. We also found no direct precedent for Focus C.

The three focus questions.
  1. Two models talking to each other. When two models hold a long conversation, does either drift away from the Assistant, and does one pull the other?
  2. Persona contagion. If a stable Assistant talks with a model pinned to a strong persona (for example a pirate or a conspiracy theorist), does the Assistant drift toward that persona?
  3. Agents with assigned roles. Multi-agent frameworks give models roles like critic, CEO, or adversary. Does role assignment pull agents off the axis, and does that predict failures or broken rules?
Method.
  1. Shared infrastructure (whole team, first): a conversation runner for two or more speakers; per-speaker projections onto each model's own axis with ConversationAnalyzer; a degeneration measure such as repetition; a fixed set of seeds and opening prompts.
  2. Focus A: first replicate Li et al.'s instruction drift on Gemma with their protocol (two system-prompted speakers, a probe question every round, a stability score). Then add the axis: plot each speaker's projection over turns and test whether it tracks, or runs ahead of, the behavioural stability score.
  3. Focus B: Li et al. saw an agent take on its partner's system prompt; test whether that shows up in activations. Pair a normal Assistant with a persona-prompted partner, track both projections, check whether the Assistant moves toward the partner persona's vector (cosine similarity), and use lagged correlation to test whether one model's drift follows the other's.
  4. Focus C: run a small multi-agent task with assigned roles, project each agent's activations, and compare them against task success and rule-following.
  5. Write-up: one paper with a shared methods section and a results section per focus.
Constraints.
  • Memory. The cheapest version is one model playing every side, which needs only one model in memory. Two different model families needs two small models (for example Gemma 2 2B and Qwen 3 1.7B or 4B) or a larger GPU. Every agent you want to measure must be an open-weights model you run yourself.
  • Degeneration. Model-to-model conversations often collapse into loops or mutual flattery after a few dozen turns. That could itself be a finding, but the team needs a way to tell drift apart from degeneration.
  • Replication. Use several random seeds and opening prompts. One conversation is an anecdote.
  • Causality (Focus B). Drift might come from the conversation's topics rather than the partner's persona, so include a control partner that covers the same topics with a neutral persona.
  • Setup cost (Focus C). Needs a task with a clear success measure, which is a big setup cost. Best owned by members with agent-framework experience.
  • Coordination. Agree on the shared infrastructure and data format before splitting up, and start Focus C's task design early since it takes longest.
Further reading.
  • Li et al. (2024). Measuring and Controlling Instruction (In)Stability in Language Model Dialogs. The starting point for Focus A and B. Two system-prompted chatbots drift from their own instructions within about eight rounds and begin adopting their partner's, measured from behaviour only. Replicate this, then add the axis.
  • Cho et al. (2025). Herd Behavior: Investigating Peer Influence in LLM-based Multi-Agent Systems. Agents conform to their peers, and how much depends on relative confidence. Behavioural evidence for Focus B.
  • Li et al. (2025). From Single to Societal: Analyzing Persona-Induced Bias in Multi-Agent Interactions. Assigned personas change trust and insistence between agents, with in-group conformity.
  • Cemri et al. (2025). Why Do Multi-Agent LLM Systems Fail?. A taxonomy of 14 failure modes across 1,600+ traces, including agents disobeying their role. A ready-made failure measure for Focus C.
  • Li et al. (2023). CAMEL: Communicative Agents for “Mind” Exploration of Large Language Model Society. Role-playing agent pairs, which report role flipping and infinite conversation loops (the degeneration problem).
  • Lee & Tiwari (2024). Prompt Infection: LLM-to-LLM Prompt Injection within Multi-Agent Systems. Malicious prompts spread from agent to agent: contagion at the text level, which you would study at the activation level.

2 The axis as an early warning for jailbreaks

SafetyTeam of 2 to 4

Question. Do jailbreak prompts push activations off the Assistant end of the axis before the model produces anything harmful?

Method.
  1. Take jailbreak prompts and benign prompts from a public benchmark.
  2. Project the activations on each prompt (before any response is generated) onto the axis.
  3. Treat the projection as a classifier score and report an ROC curve and its area.
Content guidelines for this project
  • Use existing public benchmark prompts only: JailbreakBench (its JBB-Behaviors dataset has matched harmful and benign behaviors, which suits an ROC curve) or HarmBench. Both are public and MIT-licensed.
  • Do not write new harmful prompts.
  • Do not publish or share harmful model completions, including in slides, notebooks you make public, or chat. Report scores and aggregate results only.
  • Prefer measuring the prompt before generation, so you rarely need to produce completions at all.
Constraints.
  • Role-play jailbreaks will obviously move the projection. The interesting question is whether non-role-play attacks move it too, so report the two kinds separately.
Further reading.
  • Arditi et al. (2024). Refusal in Language Models Is Mediated by a Single Direction. Jailbreak suffixes work partly by suppressing a single refusal direction, so a linear read-out can see attacks.
  • Qian et al. (2024). HSF: Defending against Jailbreak Attacks with Hidden State Filtering. Jailbreak, safe and harmful prompts cluster separately in hidden states before any generation, which is the same pre-generation setup as this idea.
  • Kirch et al. (2024). What Features in Prompts Jailbreak LLMs? Investigating the Mechanisms Behind Attacks. Probes predict jailbreak success but transfer poorly across attack families. Expect the axis to catch some attacks and not others.
  • Shah et al. (2023). Scalable and Transferable Black-Box Jailbreaks for Language Models via Persona Modulation. Persona-based jailbreaks, the role-play family the axis should catch most easily.
  • Chao et al. (2024). JailbreakBench: An Open Robustness Benchmark for Jailbreaking Large Language Models. The benchmark to use: matched harmful and benign behaviours.

Builds on projection onto the axis: Section 1 (concept 5) and Section 2 (concept 6).

3 Who the user is

SafetyTeam of 2 to 3

Question. Keeping the model's own persona fixed, does the user's identity (a child, an expert, someone in distress, a flatterer) move the model along the axis, and does that track sycophancy?

Method.
  1. Hold the system prompt fixed and vary only the user's described identity.
  2. Measure projections onto the axis for each identity.
  3. Score responses for sycophancy with a judge and compare.
Constraints.
  • The team must define up front how to separate "the model adapts its tone", which is fine, from "the model loses its identity", which is the worrying case.
  • The paper varies user behaviour to create realistic conversations, but never treats user identity as the variable under study, so this is new ground.
Further reading.
  • Ghandeharioun et al. (2024). Who’s asking? User personas and the mechanics of latent misalignment. Whether a model refuses depends strongly on who it thinks the user is, and the user persona’s geometry in activations predicts the effect. The closest precedent.
  • Chen et al. (2024). Designing a Dashboard for Transparency and Control of Conversational AI. An open model linearly encodes the user’s age, gender, education and socioeconomic status, so user identity is readable from activations.
  • Sharma et al. (2023). Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models. Sycophancy is widespread in assistants; useful definitions and evaluation formats for the judge.
  • Perez et al. (2022). Discovering Language Model Behaviors with Model-Written Evaluations. Models echo the views a user’s self-description implies. Early evidence that described identity changes answers.
  • Chen et al. (2025). Persona Vectors: Monitoring and Controlling Character Traits in Language Models. Includes a sycophancy direction you could compare with the axis.

Builds on Section 2: drift monitoring (concept 6).

4 Personality structure in persona space

Cognitive scienceTeam of 2 to 3

Question. The paper focuses on the first principal component. Do the next components (PC2 through PC5) line up with human personality dimensions such as the Big Five? This connects to the lexical hypothesis in psychology, the idea that important personality differences end up encoded in everyday language.

Method.
  1. Fit PCA with at least five components (the notebook uses two).
  2. Have a judge rate each persona on the Big Five traits.
  3. Correlate those ratings with each persona's position on PC2 through PC5.
Constraints.
  • Interpreting components is subjective. Decide in advance what counts as a match, and compare against shuffled ratings as a baseline.
  • The workshop run's 53 personas are enough for a first look at five components, but more personas make the later components more trustworthy. The paper's 275 roles are one flag away (USE_FULL_PERSONA_LIST) if you have the GPU time.
  • The paper only gives loose readings of PC2 and PC3 (Section 2.2, Table 1; for Gemma, PC2 is roughly "informal or creative" versus "systematic") and never tests the Big Five, so this is new.
Further reading.
  • Cutler & Condon (2022). Deep Lexical Hypothesis: Identifying personality structure in natural language. Language-model adjective similarities recover the first factors of human personality structure, which supports the lexical-hypothesis framing (Neuroticism and Openness were weaker).
  • Serapio-García et al. (2023). Personality Traits in Large Language Models. Psychometrically valid Big Five measurement in LLM outputs, stronger for larger instruction-tuned models. Use it to design the judge’s ratings.
  • Jiang et al. (2022). Evaluating and Inducing Personality in Pre-trained Language Models. A Big Five inventory for models, and prompting that induces specific traits.
  • Allbert et al. (2024). Identifying and Manipulating Personality Traits in LLMs Through Activation Engineering. Personality traits correspond to directions in activation space, using the same toolkit as this lab.

Builds on Section 1: PCA and the Assistant Axis (concept 5).

5 Narrator distance

Cognitive scienceTeam of 2 to 3

Question. Does writing a character in third person ("the villain said") keep the model nearer the Assistant end than writing it in first person ("I am the villain")? This gets at the difference between simulating a character and becoming one.

Method.
  1. Build matched prompts that differ only in perspective.
  2. Extract response activations as in Section 1 and project them onto the axis.
  3. Compare the two perspectives, persona by persona.
Constraints.
  • Prompts must be tightly matched, and the content of the responses must stay comparable, or the comparison is not fair.
Further reading.
  • Shanahan et al. (2023). Role-Play with Large Language Models. The simulator versus simulacra framing: the model as an author voicing characters, not becoming them.
  • Li et al. (2023). DeepInception: Hypnotize Large Language Model to Be Jailbreaker. Nested third-person fiction changes safety behaviour, so narrative framing measurably matters.
  • Wei et al. (2023). Jailbroken: How Does LLM Safety Training Fail?. Explains why fictional and role-play framings slip past safety training (competing objectives).
  • Lu et al. (2026). The Assistant Axis. Role-play and persona prompts pull the model off the axis. Nobody has yet compared first and third person, so this is untested.

Builds on Section 1: persona vectors (concept 3 and concept 4).

6 Language and culture

Cognitive scienceTeam of 2 to 4, ideally with native speakers

Question. If the axis is built from English prompts, does it still separate personas in Spanish, Mandarin, or Arabic? Is the Assistant one character across languages, or a different one in each?

Method.
  1. Translate the personas and questions.
  2. Extract activations and project them onto the English axis.
  3. Also build a separate axis in each language and compare it with the English one by cosine similarity (same model, so this is allowed).
Constraints.
  • Judge quality drops outside English, and Gemma's ability varies by language.
  • Translated prompts need review by native speakers.
Further reading.
  • Wendler et al. (2024). Do Llamas Work in English? On the Latent Language of Multilingual Transformers. Middle layers work in an English-leaning concept space, so an English axis plausibly transfers.
  • Schut et al. (2025). Do Multilingual LLMs Think In English?. Steering vectors computed in English work better than ones computed in the input language. Direct support for the transfer test.
  • Wang et al. (2025). Refusal Direction is Universal Across Safety-Aligned Languages. A refusal direction from English transfers across 14 languages, and directions from different languages are nearly parallel. The template for your cosine comparison.
  • Deng et al. (2023). Multilingual Jailbreak Challenges in Large Language Models. Safety is weaker in lower-resource languages, a reason the Assistant might be “less itself” outside English.
  • AlKhamissi et al. (2024). Investigating Cultural Alignment of Large Language Models. Prompting language shifts a model’s cultural alignment, so the Assistant may differ by language.

Builds on Section 1: persona vectors and PCA (concept 3 and concept 5).

What your proposal must answer

Whether you choose a card or your own idea, your proposal should answer each item, briefly:

  1. Research question, in one sentence.
  2. Which notebook method it builds on (name the section and functions).
  3. Which model, and how it fits in memory on Kaggle's T4 x2.
  4. The control or baseline that rules out the obvious confound.
  5. Rough OpenRouter cost: how many judge calls, which judge model, and an estimate in dollars.
  6. What counts as success or failure: the result that would support your idea, and the result that would count against it.
  7. Team: members, and who owns which part.

A strong proposal changes one thing at a time, makes a prediction that could turn out wrong, includes a comparison, and is small enough to show a first result within two weeks on free hardware.
