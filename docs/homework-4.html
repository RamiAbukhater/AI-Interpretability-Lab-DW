What the proposal must include

This builds on the Homework 2 checklist, with a prediction, data, a timeline, and a fallback added. Answer each item briefly.

  1. Title and team: members, sub-group, and who owns which part.
  2. Research question, in one sentence.
  3. Prediction: what you expect to see, and the result that would show you were wrong.
  4. Method: the notebook section and functions it builds on, and the steps in order.
  5. Model, and how it fits in memory on Kaggle's T4 x2.
  6. Data: which prompts, personas, conversations or benchmark, and how many.
  7. The control or baseline that rules out the obvious confound.
  8. Rough OpenRouter cost: how many judge calls, which judge model, and an estimate in dollars.
  9. Timeline: what your first result on November 22 will be, and what goes in the paper by November 29.
  10. Risks and fallback: what is most likely to go wrong, and the smaller result you will report if it does.

As before: change one thing at a time, make a prediction that could turn out wrong, and include a comparison.

Notes for the multi-agent team

Full details are on the idea card.

Shared infrastructure section (whole team)
  • Conversation runner: who builds it and by when. Aim for a working version by Friday, November 20, since both sub-groups depend on it. The cheapest version runs one Gemma 2 9B playing both speakers.
  • Data format: agree on it now. For example, one row per speaker per turn, with the conversation id, seed, opening prompt, turn number, speaker, system prompt or persona, the response text, and its projection onto the axis.
  • Seeds and opening prompts: one fixed set that every experiment uses.
  • Degeneration check: how you will tell drift apart from conversations collapsing into loops or mutual flattery (for example a repetition score).
  • Focus A. Replicate Li et al.'s instruction drift first, with their public code and protocol (a probe question every round, a stability score), then add each speaker's projection with ConversationAnalyzer. Your key comparison: does the projection track, or move before, the behavioural score?
  • Focus B. Your control is a partner that covers the same topics with a neutral persona, so you can tell the partner's persona apart from the conversation's topics. Say which personas you will test, and how you will measure movement toward the partner (cosine similarity with the partner persona's vector, lagged correlation between the two projections).
  • Focus C (only if you take it on). Name the task and its success measure up front. Cemri et al. (2025) give a ready-made list of failure modes to score against.

Notes for the jailbreak team

Full details are on the idea card.

  • Data. Use JailbreakBench's JBB-Behaviors, which has matched harmful and benign behaviours, plus its published attack prompts. Say which attack families you will use, and label each one as role-play or not.
  • Measure the prompt, not the answer. Take activations on the prompt before any response is generated. That keeps you within the content guidelines and means you may need almost no judge calls, so your cost estimate could be close to zero. Say so if it is.
  • Baseline. An ROC curve means little on its own. Compare the axis against at least one simple baseline, for example prompt length, or the refusal direction from Arditi et al. (2024).
  • Report role-play and non-role-play attacks separately. Role-play attacks will obviously move the projection; the interesting result is whether the others do too.
  • Content guidelines. State in the proposal that you will use public benchmark prompts only, will not write new harmful prompts, and will report only scores and aggregates.
